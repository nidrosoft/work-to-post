## Work to Post

Help the current user turn material they can substantiate into useful posts for a specific reader. Optimize for qualified conversations about work. Treat reach as a separate signal. The templates are adaptable writing aids, not evidence of guaranteed virality or conversion.

### Start from the current person

Use the user's supplied profile and current instructions. Never inherit the identity, clients, experience, voice, metrics, or availability of someone in an example. A first-time engineer can use the same framework as a senior consultant without claiming the same history.

Separate these choices:

- **Outcome:** CLIENTS means a buyer engaging the user's business or independent practice for a scoped service. OFFERS means a team approaching the person for an embedded consulting assignment, contract, or employment role. Let the user define an ambiguous arrangement. BOTH can describe the audience fit, but choose one primary ask for each post.
- **Reader:** one recognizable buyer, practitioner, or hiring decision-maker.
- **Topic:** the area of knowledge, such as AI architecture, backend engineering, design, or business operations.
- **Use case:** the concrete situation, such as a support team reviewing generated replies.
- **Angle:** the reason this particular post is useful. Choose from [the angle library](#the-15-angle-library).
- **Format and account capability:** short post, longer single post, thread, reply, or media caption. Use the current user's account capability and preference, then choose the form that fits the point and evidence. A Premium account does not imply a short-post preference.

The same topic can support several angles. The same angle can work across many topics. A series is also a packaging choice and can contain demos, diagnoses, or decisions. Do not force mutually exclusive research labels onto ordinary writing.

Natural language is enough. Do not require users to memorize mode names or fill every field. Default to Draft when they bring material and ask for a post. Keep a profile reusable by returning a copy the user can save. Do not imply memory persists across products or new chats.

### From notes to a draft

1. **Extract the material.** Identify what happened or was noticed, the user's role, evidence, intended reader, and desired outcome. Use the profile for stable context. A newer explicit user correction overrides an older profile.
2. **Distinguish claim types.** Separate user-supplied firsthand facts, externally sourced claims, interpretations, future plans, and hypothetical examples. User-supplied facts can support a draft without pretending they were independently verified. Link external claims to sources actually inspected. An inaccessible link alone does not verify its contents.
3. **Select an angle that the evidence supports.** Use the library's minimum material and boundaries. Explain the choice in one sentence. When the user explicitly requests an angle that needs missing material, name the gap and offer an honestly labeled concept, plan, or fill-in draft. Do not silently turn a proposed test into a completed benchmark.
4. **Write one useful point.** Lead with a concrete action, situation, finding, or decision. Include the mechanism or consequence that makes it useful. Use the selected structure as a guide, not a mandatory sentence pattern. Keep implementation details when the intended reader needs them.
5. **Choose proof and a next step.** Distinguish a supplied artifact from one still to create. A useful standalone insight can end without a sales ask. When asking for action, choose one that matches the reader and outcome. Avoid comment-to-unlock bait or invented scarcity.
6. **Edit quietly before returning.** Apply the complete [editorial pass](#editorial-pass) to every draft, variation, and revision. It embeds the No AI Slop editing rules with the workflow adaptations described there. Use the draft and profile already in context. Do not announce the pass or append an editing report unless the user asks for one.
7. **Recheck the edited copy.** Use [the review checks](#review-the-draft-and-its-results). Confirm that editing preserved facts, uncertainty, attribution, technical meaning, and the intended ask. Check the final length. A missing result stays missing.

For a normal draft, return:

- **Choice:** intended reader, primary outcome, angle, and one-sentence reason.
- **Post:** clean copy, separate from editorial notes.
- **Supporting material:** a supplied link or a clearly labeled media/reply plan.
- **Readiness:** ready for user review, needs a specific fact, or needs a specific asset. Identify only material gaps. Ready for review is not a prediction of performance or permission to publish.

If the user requests copy only, omit editorial commentary and respect the requested length and format. Copy only does not mean short copy. If they request all 15 angles, provide the requested examples with their formats and intended outcomes. For publication drafts, identify evidence gaps. For an explicitly requested private preview, follow the preview rules below.

### Evidence and voice

For publication drafts, never invent clients, roles, screenshots, repositories, conversations, measurements, test results, dates of availability, or outcomes. Label hypothetical examples in a way that survives copying the post. When a fictional story would read as the user's real experience, provide a labeled scenario or request the actual facts.

For an explicitly requested private demonstration, the user may authorize fully filled invented names, stories, conversations, and measurements. Keep one clear note outside the sample copy when the user wants an uninterrupted preview, instead of repeating labels inside every post. Explain that the invented scenarios are illustrative and do not establish actual client work. Do not attach invented testimonials or achievements to identifiable real third parties. Do not save preview details as facts in the user's profile or later turn them into publication-ready claims. The angle library's evidence requirements still apply before any real publication.

Do not turn another person's story into the user's firsthand account. Preserve attribution when summarizing research. A one-person observation can be valuable when its scope stays clear. A search-selected set of posts cannot measure market adoption.

The [editorial pass](#editorial-pass) governs writing quality. Preserve substantive technical terms, official names, direct quotations, and the user's own voice. Writing quality is judged by the copy and its support, not an AI-authorship score.

### Choose the format and length

Use account capability and preferences already supplied; do not ask a Premium user to repeat them. If they are unknown and materially affect the draft, ask once: "Do you have X Premium or longer-post access, and would you prefer one post, a thread, or my recommendation?" Keep working on the substance while awaiting the answer. Do not interrupt an edit with an explicit length or a request for an ordinary short post to ask about subscriptions.

- **Standard account:** keep each published post within the standard limit. If the story needs more room, offer a thread instead of dropping context, evidence, or qualifications.
- **Premium or confirmed longer-post access:** allow a complete story or explanation. A useful starting range is roughly 150–400 words for a single post; length follows the material, not a quota. A brief can still be short when it earns that length.
- **Thread:** use distinct numbered posts when a sequence, investigation, comparison, or tutorial benefits from separate steps. Start with the situation and stakes; move through evidence, decisions, outcome, and remaining limits as appropriate. Each part must add something. Do not split a paragraph merely to create more posts, repeat the hook in each part, or end every part with a cliffhanger. Premium thread parts can exceed the standard limit when needed.
- **Long single post:** keep one connected argument or story together. Use readable paragraphs and short section labels only when they help. Do not make every paragraph a one-line punchline. A numbered series can contain either long posts or threads; it is separate from format.

If the user requests all 15 angles for a Premium account, choose a mix of long posts and threads rather than forcing every angle into 280 characters. A request for a full story should retain the scene, people, constraint, decision, consequence, and reflection that make it understandable.

X's official help lists a standard 280-character limit and longer posts of up to 25,000 characters as checked on November 13, 2026. Treat this as a ceiling, not a writing target, and recheck official guidance if the account, composer, or policy differs. [Official post types](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/types-of-posts), [Premium longer posts](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/x-premium-how-to).

Templates contain variables whose expansion changes length. Count the final copy and each thread part, including numbering, using the platform's link and Unicode rules when a reliable counter is available. Do not claim an exact platform count from an ordinary character count. Keep essential qualifications with the claim they limit.

### Research and actions

Draft from supplied material without requiring paid research, API keys, or a particular connector. When current facts or an explicit research request require lookup, use available authorized tools, inspect the underlying sources, and record date and scope. Treg is an optional research source when connected. Observe its current pricing and authorization requirements before paid calls. If lookup is unavailable, ask for source text or return a draft with the claim withheld.

Read [research notes](#research-context-and-limits) only when explaining the framework's evidence or planning a refresh. Use [worked examples](#worked-examples) to understand application, never as facts about a new user.

This workflow prepares material for review. Publishing, scheduling, replying to people, messaging, and changing external profiles require authorization for those actions. A request to draft or plan alone does not supply it.

## The 15 angle library

These are editorial choices. A01 through A15 are stable IDs for this kit, separate from the older research taxonomy. They are not a ranking or 15 compulsory weekly posts. A07 can wrap another angle as a numbered series. Choose one primary contribution for an individual post and record the series separately when useful.

Use the structures to shape supplied material. Replace brackets with supported facts. Omit irrelevant template lines rather than filling them with generic claims. A direct sales ask is optional except when availability is the point of the post.

### Find an angle from the material

| Material available | Start with | Distinguishing question |
|---|---|---|
| A functioning feature | A01 Demo | What can a person do now? |
| A delivered client engagement | A02 Deliverable | What did the buyer need and receive? |
| A meaningful implementation decision | A03 Case study | Why was it built this way? |
| A proposed alternative to an existing flow | A04 Redesign | What would change, and how would you test it? |
| A real work window | A05 Availability | What specific work can someone engage you for? |
| A usable checklist, template, or tool | A06 Resource | What can the reader use today? |
| A recurring subject with another useful installment | A07 Series | What is new in this installment? |
| A repeated behavior | A08 Observation | What recurs, and under what conditions? |
| Comparable data across time or groups | A09 Trend | What changed, or differs, and how was it measured? |
| One encounter that changed your understanding | A10 Field note | What did this moment change in your next decision? |
| Recorded tests of alternatives | A11 Comparison | How do approaches differ on a shared task? |

### A01 Shipped build demo

**Use:** A feature or product works now. CLIENTS readers should recognize the business task. OFFERS readers should see the author's contribution and relevant engineering or design work.

**Minimum material:** What works, the author's actual role, a complete action and result, and a demonstrable artifact. A prototype can qualify if labeled with its actual status.

**Structure:** Useful action → what was built → input to result → inspect or try.

> [User] can now [action].
>
> I built [feature] to handle [specific problem].
>
> Here is [input] becoming [usable result], including [important verification or recovery step].
>
> [Demo or next step.]

**Proof and limits:** Show a complete action at readable speed. Label mocked data and unfinished behavior when it affects the claim. Do not call it newly launched unless that is true. Use observable behavior if performance has not been measured.

**Possible subjects:** A data import, mobile interaction, internal operations tool, AI document review, or accessible navigation.

### A02 Client deliverable showcase

**Use:** A buyer received a specific deliverable. Prioritize CLIENTS. OFFERS fit when the author's role and delivery judgment are visible.

**Minimum material:** A real engagement, shareable scope, the user's contribution, and a verified description of what was delivered. Respect existing limits on client disclosure.

**Structure:** Buyer situation → constraint → delivered work → observable change → similar problem invitation.

> [Customer type] needed [job] while dealing with [constraint].
>
> I delivered [scope], including [consequential detail].
>
> They can now [supported change].
>
> If your team faces [same problem], [single contact action].

**Proof and limits:** Use shareable footage, an approved case study, or attributed feedback. Specify the measurement conditions for any numerical result. An independent concept belongs in A04.

**Possible subjects:** CRM integration, MVP delivery, web migration, AI rollout, or internal dashboard.

### A03 Process case study

**Use:** Explain why a project was built a particular way. OFFERS readers can evaluate ownership and trade-offs. CLIENTS readers can understand a decision that affects their operations.

**Minimum material:** One actual project, a constraint, a real decision, and the known implementation or verification. Mark an untested design as proposed.

**Structure:** Constraint → considered options → chosen approach → implementation → verification and remaining trade-off.

> In [project], [constraint] made [decision] important.
>
> I chose [approach] because [reason].
>
> It connects [components or steps] in this way: [mechanism].
>
> [Test or observation] supports [bounded result]. The remaining trade-off is [limitation].

**Proof and limits:** Show the specific flow, diff, diagram, or test. Describe proposed verification as future work. A general repeatable workflow without a project can fit A06 or A14 better.

**Possible subjects:** Database writes, state recovery, deployment, document regeneration, or choosing native versus web behavior.

### A04 Unsolicited redesign

**Use:** Offer an independent alternative to a recognizable interaction. CLIENTS readers see a customer problem addressed. OFFERS readers see reasoning and implementation skill.

**Minimum material:** An actual existing flow or clearly described generic scenario, the proposed change, and a testable expectation. A visual is helpful but a specific concept can stand alone.

**Structure:** Existing friction → concept label → alternative → expected behavior → test.

> Concept for [flow].
>
> Today, [specific friction].
>
> I would change [interaction] so [user] can [action].
>
> I would test [outcome] and check [possible downside].

**Proof and limits:** Distinguish an existing prototype from a proposed one. An unsolicited concept does not establish affiliation with a named company or an improvement in conversion. In a mobile interaction, demonstrate the behavior being claimed.

**Possible subjects:** Quote forms, onboarding, AI review screens, data imports, or Figma-to-code interaction details.

### A05 Specific availability

**Use:** Make a real opportunity to work together concrete. Choose CLIENTS for a scoped service or OFFERS for a role or personal assignment.

**Minimum material:** The actual scope or role sought, a truthful time window when stated, and a relevant work sample or accurately described experience.

**Structure:** Availability → specific problem or responsibility → relevant proof → contact route.

> From [actual date], I am available for [scope or engagement].
>
> I help [reader] with [specific problem], and can own [responsibility].
>
> [Relevant work sample or factual experience.]
>
> If you need [defined contribution], [contact action].

**Proof and limits:** Omit a date when unknown. Do not invent limited slots, years of experience, employers, or an immediate start. Asking to open conversations can be accurate without claiming immediate availability.

**Possible subjects:** A backend contract, an AI architecture review, a product engineering role, or a scoped website build.

### A06 Free tool or template

**Use:** Give readers something they can use. CLIENTS readers can prepare or diagnose a project. OFFERS readers can assess how the author helps a team work.

**Minimum material:** A usable resource in the post or an accessible artifact. A short checklist counts. A proposed resource is a plan, not a release.

**Structure:** Specific job → resource → shortest way to use it → example or limit.

> A [checklist/template/tool] for [task].
>
> [Usable content or direct access.]
>
> Use it when [situation]. Start with [first action].
>
> [Relevant limitation or filled example.]

**Proof and limits:** Deliver what the copy promises. Do not invent a GitHub repo or working download. Avoid comment-to-unlock gates. A large resource can be linked with a small useful excerpt.

**Possible subjects:** Component handoff, deployment checklist, AI evaluation sheet, discovery questions, or a cost calculator.

### A07 Numbered series

**Use:** Build continuity around a real subject. CLIENTS readers follow recurring business problems. OFFERS readers follow evolving work and reasoning.

**Minimum material:** A useful installment and its current evidence. The first installment is enough to start. A promised future episode is not completed work.

**Structure:** Useful change or point → series identity → evidence or explanation → optional next installment.

> [Useful change or observation.]
>
> [Series name], [number].
>
> [What changed, what you learned, or what the reader can do.]
>
> [Artifact or next check.]

**Proof and limits:** Each installment should work on its own. Record the underlying angle when useful, such as A12 inside a reliability series. Vary the material and question rather than retelling the same result with a new number.

**Possible subjects:** Founder infrastructure, production AI notes, component implementation, or small-business workflow repairs.

### A08 Pattern observation

**Use:** Explain a recurring behavior. CLIENTS readers recognize a repeated operational problem. OFFERS readers assess understanding of systems, products, or users.

**Minimum material:** Independent examples, repeated firsthand observations, or an attributed source that documents repetition. Keep the scope as narrow as the evidence.

**Structure:** Pattern → concrete examples → possible explanation → practical implication → boundary.

> Across [bounded experiences or sources], [behavior] keeps appearing.
>
> [Examples or source.]
>
> My interpretation is [explanation]. That suggests [action].
>
> I would check [counterexample or limiting condition].

**Proof and limits:** Separate observation from explanation. One anecdote supports a narrow field note rather than a whole-market claim. A memorable term is optional and should not imply an invented concept is widely established.

**Possible subjects:** Coding-agent handoffs, manual workarounds, repeated onboarding confusion, or how teams use AI-generated drafts.

### A09 Data-backed trend

**Use:** Explain change over time or a meaningful difference between comparable groups. CLIENTS readers get a planning implication. OFFERS readers see careful research judgment.

**Minimum material:** A measured quantity, population or denominator, period, comparable groups or dates, and inspected source or supplied dataset.

**Structure:** Finding → population and period → comparison → interpretation → practical consequence.

> [Source] measured [metric] among [population] during [period].
>
> [Group or period A] showed [result], compared with [B].
>
> My interpretation is [bounded inference].
>
> For [reader], I would consider [action], with [important limit].

**Proof and limits:** Keep changes in measurement definitions visible. Planned adoption and actual use are different measures. Search results, viral posts, and likes cannot by themselves measure adoption. Do not extrapolate a small or selected sample to everyone.

**Possible subjects:** Adoption by business size, support behavior, deployment patterns, or comparable user cohorts.

### A10 Firsthand field note

**Use:** One encounter changes the next decision. Good for both CLIENTS and OFFERS involving discovery, product ownership, or customer-facing engineering.

**Minimum material:** A real encounter, observation, or feedback the author received, a specific detail, and its consequence. Attribute secondhand accounts instead of adopting them as a memory.

**Structure:** Moment → revealing detail → reconsidered assumption → changed action.

> During [real encounter], [detail] caught my attention.
>
> I had been thinking about [initial problem].
>
> That made me reconsider [assumption].
>
> The next thing I [did/would check] was [action].

**Proof and limits:** Use the tense that matches what happened. A proposed next check stays conditional. Preserve confidentiality without making a composite story look like a single real client event.

**Possible subjects:** Customer onboarding, operator interviews, a support call, or what a user did differently from the prototype's expected path.

### A11 Controlled comparison

**Use:** Compare alternatives on a shared task. OFFERS readers inspect method and trade-offs. CLIENTS readers need the connection to cost, time, quality, or reliability.

**Minimum material:** Actual measurements, comparable inputs, scoring criteria, relevant versions and conditions, and known limitations. Without results, offer a proposed experiment.

**Structure:** Shared task → controlled conditions → results → trade-off → scoped recommendation.

> I compared [A/version] and [B/version] on [same task/sample].
>
> I held [conditions] constant and measured [metric].
>
> A produced [result]. B produced [result].
>
> For [this use], I would choose [option] because [reason]. [Method and limits.]

**Proof and limits:** Keep the denominator, failed cases, and relevant cost or latency visible. Repeat runs when variability could change the conclusion. A fixed-prompt test measures those configurations. One result does not establish universal model or tool superiority.

**Possible subjects:** Retrieval configurations, import implementations, queue designs, model workflows, or interface variants on the same task.

### A12 Failure postmortem

**Use:** Explain a real failure through evidence, recovery, and a change. CLIENTS readers see accountability. OFFERS readers see production ownership.

**Minimum material:** A real incident, known impact, diagnostic evidence, and what has actually been repaired or remains unresolved.

**Structure:** Failure and impact → evidence → cause → recovery → repair → verification.

> [System] failed when [trigger], affecting [verified scope].
>
> [Evidence] led us to [cause].
>
> We recovered by [action] and changed [implementation].
>
> [Test or monitoring] now checks [failure condition]. [Remaining limitation, if any.]

**Proof and limits:** Do not invent an incident to fill a calendar. A hypothetical failure can be a clearly labeled scenario in A13 or a future test in A11. Never claim recovery, a root cause, or zero recurrence before it is established.

**Possible subjects:** Duplicate records, broken migration, agent errors, failed rollout, or a missed deployment assumption.

### A13 Public help and diagnosis

**Use:** Give a useful next check for a concrete symptom. CLIENTS readers can recognize a problem they have. OFFERS readers can assess relevant troubleshooting. Works as a post or a reply.

**Minimum material:** A real reported symptom or an explicitly conditional scenario, a plausible diagnostic, and a way to interpret the result.

**Structure:** Symptom → diagnostic check → result interpretation → next action.

> If [symptom], check [point] first.
>
> Run [small diagnostic].
>
> If [result A], investigate [cause]. If [result B], check [alternative].
>
> You are trying to establish [narrow fact].

**Proof and limits:** Frame a suspected cause as a possibility until tested. Give help before an optional invitation. Do not imply you investigated someone's system when you only read their question.

**Possible subjects:** Lost leads, stale UI state, failed imports, inconsistent model output, or a form with inaccessible error feedback.

### A14 Decision rule

**Use:** Explain when a recommendation changes. CLIENTS readers get a scope or spending criterion. OFFERS readers can inspect architecture and product judgment.

**Minimum material:** A real decision, options, relevant constraints, and a reason the recommendation changes. It can be an explicitly stated professional recommendation without fabricated project history.

**Structure:** Decision → conditions favoring A → conditions favoring B → trade-off → next fact to check.

> I would choose [A] when [conditions].
>
> I would choose [B] when [different conditions].
>
> The trade-off is [consequence].
>
> Before deciding, check [fact that could change the recommendation].

**Proof and limits:** Make conditions operational enough to help a real choice. Avoid universal "always" and "never" rules unless justified. Label numerical cutoffs as heuristics when they have not been measured.

**Possible subjects:** Buy versus build, queue versus synchronous request, rules versus model, prototype versus production, or web versus native.

### A15 Forecast with a test

**Use:** State a future expectation that can be revisited. CLIENTS readers get a planning discussion. OFFERS readers see disciplined product or technical direction. Use sparingly until evidence and a test exist.

**Minimum material:** A current signal, a bounded prediction, deadline, observable measure, and a condition that would disprove it.

**Structure:** Signal → prediction and population → deadline → measurement → failure condition.

> Because [current evidence], I expect [outcome] among [defined group] by [date].
>
> I will track [observable measure].
>
> If [failure condition], I will revise the prediction.

**Proof and limits:** A forecast is a hypothesis, not present fact. Predefine how the sample is selected. A missing sample makes a test incomplete. Changing the outcome after the deadline does not confirm the original prediction. A draft does not schedule an automatic review.

**Possible subjects:** Buyer concerns, workflow adoption, a product experiment, or which technical capabilities a defined group asks for.

## Editorial pass

Apply this pass to every post draft, variant, and revision before delivery. These rules adapt Peter Yang's No AI Slop skill and evaluation checklist under the MIT license. The complete notice and source revision are in [third-party notices](#third-party-notices). All rules needed for this workflow are included here; users do not need to install another skill.

Editing should preserve the requested depth. For a longer post or thread, remove filler while keeping the scene, reasoning, evidence, and transitions that make the whole story understandable. Do not compress every post to 280 characters. For explicitly authorized private demonstrations, construct the illustrative scenario before this pass, keep its preview status clear outside the copy as requested, and edit it consistently without treating its invented details as verified facts.

### Use the draft and context already available

Read the complete draft. Identify its point, intended reader, factual claims, and the writer's vocabulary and rhythm. Use the supplied profile and writing samples. If the draft and audience already exist in context, do not ask the user to provide them again.

For ordinary drafting and editing, make the smallest changes that improve the post. Perform the checks silently. Return the agreed post output without an automatic "What changed" section, editorial score, or announcement that the text has been humanized. Explain edits only when requested or when a material claim cannot be supported.

For a request to detect or flag awkward writing without rewriting, identify concrete patterns, quote the relevant short phrases, and suggest concise fixes. Respect the request not to rewrite. Do not guess who or what authored the text.

### Preserve what makes the writing useful

- Keep the user's point, factual meaning, certainty, and voice. Do not invent a statistic, opinion, anecdote, result, quote, or client to make a sentence more concrete.
- Leave strong sentences alone. Preserve purposeful bluntness, humor, casual phrasing, and useful admissions. Do not make every paragraph equally polished or every post follow the same cadence.
- Lead with the point when the setup adds nothing. Keep a story detail when it explains the decision or makes the situation understandable.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs. Replace an abstract claim with a mechanism or fact already supplied. If the evidence is missing, remove the claim or request the specific missing fact.
- Protect details that carry meaning: denominators, dates, sample conditions, technical terminology, limitations, scope, and attribution. Keep direct quotations accurate or clearly paraphrase them.
- Split tangled sentences without flattening natural speech. Keep "I think," "maybe," or "in this test" when they express a real degree of uncertainty.
- Use the portability test: if a sentence could move unchanged into almost anyone's post, look for an actual detail that gives it a purpose here. Some short instructions are necessarily general; do not force invented specificity into them.
- Keep the writer's structure unless it gets in the way. Preserve deliberate progression, personal asides, and rhythm where they serve the point.

### Words and phrases to reconsider

Remove decorative uses of: delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game changer, this is huge, this changes everything, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, and ever-evolving.

These are editing signals, not blind search-and-replace commands. Preserve a precise technical use such as "agent harness," an official name, or a quotation. Do not replace a correct term with a vague synonym just to avoid a word on this list. In this engineering framework, precision takes priority over the upstream blanket word ban.

Cut empty qualifiers such as "simply," "truly," "fundamentally," and "importantly" when they add no meaning. Keep natural emphasis and uncertainty when they belong to the writer.

Remove delays such as "it's worth noting," "at the end of the day," "in today's world," "the reality is," "when it comes to," and "let's dive in" when the sentence works without them. Do not mechanically strip a phrase that is doing useful work in a personal story.

### Patterns to revise

1. **Binary slogans.** Replace the stock "This isn't X. It's Y" construction with the actual claim. Preserve necessary factual comparisons and negative conditions.
2. **Throat-clearing openings.** Remove "Here's the thing" or "Let me be clear" when they delay the point.
3. **Faux-insight setups.** Cut "What nobody tells you" or "The part everyone misses" unless the scope can be supported. State the observation itself.
4. **Dramatic colon reveals.** Rewrite a phrase such as "The secret: better prompts" as a normal sentence. Colons remain useful for genuine lists, labels, and quotations.
5. **Superficial analysis.** Replace trailing "highlighting," "underscoring," or "reflecting" commentary with an actual consequence, or delete it.
6. **Importance puffery.** Replace claims that something is pivotal or transformative with what actually changed.
7. **Reader-directed commentary.** Remove "That matters more than it sounds," "As you can see," and similar lines that tell readers how to interpret a point already made.
8. **Vague attribution.** Replace "experts agree" or "studies show" with an inspected source and its actual finding. If a source is missing, narrow or withhold the claim.
9. **Inflated verbs.** Prefer a direct verb to phrases such as "serves as a centralized hub for." Say what the product does.
10. **Synonym cycling.** Use the same accurate term consistently. Do not rotate between agent, assistant, and tool merely for variety.
11. **Negative listing.** Replace a sequence of "Not X. Not Y. Z" with a useful positive description.
12. **Dramatic fragments.** Remove stacked punchlines that add emphasis without information. Keep genuine fragments that fit the user's voice and remain clear.
13. **Robotic rhythm.** Vary sentence and paragraph shape when the thought calls for it. Do not force every post into the same three-line cadence.
14. **Rhetorical setups.** Remove "What if I told you," "Plot twist," and self-answered questions that manufacture suspense.
15. **Fake-profound endings.** Delete the slogan or metaphor that tries to make the ending sound deep. End on a concrete consequence, useful next action, or the last supported point.
16. **Recap endings.** Remove a closing summary that repeats a short post. The reader does not need the same point twice.
17. **Decorative formatting.** Use headings, bullets, bold, and emojis only when they help the intended reader. Do not decorate short copy into a generic template.
18. **Habitual em dashes.** Prefer ordinary punctuation in short posts. Preserve punctuation when it expresses the user's voice and improves clarity.

### Final silent check

Before returning the edited draft, check:

1. The point, facts, attribution, and degree of certainty are unchanged.
2. No concrete detail was invented to replace generic language.
3. The copy sounds like this writer addressing this reader, rather than a uniform brand voice imposed on everyone.
4. Useful edge, humor, technical precision, and natural cadence survived the edit.
5. Generic introductions, vague importance claims, automatic contrast slogans, and decorative endings are gone where they added no information.
6. The post still has enough evidence and context for its chosen angle.
7. The ask, if present, is specific and appropriate; there is no added sales pitch by default.
8. The final format and length fit the request. Editing did not remove a required qualification to make the copy shorter.
9. An asset promised in the copy exists, or the post is explicitly marked as needing that asset.
10. The response follows the user's requested output. Ordinary drafting does not expose this checklist or append a change report. A detection-only request receives findings without a rewrite.

Fix failed checks and reread the final copy. If a failure comes from missing evidence, identify that gap instead of polishing it into certainty. Continue with [the final review](#review-the-draft-and-its-results) for claim, proof, and outcome checks.

## A sustainable posting and review rhythm

The goal is a steady supply of useful material and a record of the conversations it creates. Select a cadence the person can maintain. Fifteen angles are choices for a backlog, not a requirement to publish fifteen posts or cover every angle each week.

### Capture while working

Keep a short note when something becomes useful to explain. Record the situation, reader, specific fact, artifact, and what it changed. Examples include a working feature, an unexpected user action, a deployment failure, a customer question, or a source that changes a recommendation.

Keep facts and interpretation distinguishable. Save measurement conditions alongside numbers. Record whether a screenshot or customer detail can be shared. A five-line note captured during work is usually more useful than trying to reconstruct a story later.

### Choose a week's material

Review the backlog once a week. Select pieces with a clear reader and enough evidence. A suggested starting cadence is three posts, adjusted to actual capacity:

| Slot | Reader value | Possible angles |
|---|---|---|
| Show | Let someone inspect working software or delivered work | A01, A02, A03 |
| Explain | Help someone understand a pattern, decision, or field experience | A08, A09, A10, A11, A12, A14, A15 |
| Help | Give a usable resource, diagnosis, or specific concept | A04, A06, A13 |

Use A05 when real availability is the relevant news. A07 can carry a continuing subject through these slots. If only one piece is strong, publish one. Reserve a slot for a timely question or genuine development instead of filling every day with weaker material.

Across several weeks, review whether the chosen topics represent the work the person wants more of. A broad professional can rotate engineering, AI, consulting, and design while retaining a recognizable reader or set of problems. There is no evidence-based requirement for an exact topic percentage.

### Turn one use case into several distinct posts

Suppose the material is an actual lead intake integration:

- A01 shows a submitted request reaching the CRM.
- A03 explains a verified implementation choice.
- A10 describes a real operator detail that changed the scope.
- A13 gives a conditional diagnostic for a missing record.
- A14 explains when an existing connector is sufficient.

Those are separate questions. A12 requires an actual incident. A11 requires a recorded comparison. A09 requires comparable data. Do not manufacture those just because they appear in the library. A second post should add a fact, decision, method, or audience-specific implication.

### Draft and prepare proof

Complete [the four-line brief](#a-brief-for-one-post) or paste notes. Ask the assistant to select an angle and draft one post. Review the claim and voice, then prepare the actual media or source reply it needs. Keep any essential caveat in the published copy or thread.

Publication is a separate action. A content plan does not authorize an assistant to post, schedule, or message people.

### Learn from what happened

Record the URL, angle, reader, topic, proof, and primary outcome. Take engagement snapshots around 48 hours and seven days as a convenient starting routine. Review qualified opportunities again later because a buyer or hiring conversation can take longer.

Use [the opportunity log](#post-and-opportunity-log) to separate CLIENTS from OFFERS and record exact-post, series, or profile attribution. Responding to relevant questions can reveal the next useful post. The user can do this themselves or explicitly ask the assistant to draft a reply.

At the end of a four-week pilot, review:

1. Which topics attracted the people you wanted to reach?
2. Which artifacts led to substantive questions or work conversations?
3. Which angles were easiest to support with new real material?
4. What did prospects say they saw, and what remains unattributed?
5. Which one change should the next cycle test?

Keep useful exceptions and repeated evidence. Do not rewrite the whole framework because one post performed poorly. A small initial pilot helps choose what to repeat; it cannot establish that a format caused an outcome.

### Requests to reuse

> Plan three posts from these notes for the coming week. Cover the strongest supported angles, make the intended reader clear, and list any missing proof. Use fewer posts if the material does not support three.

> Review these published posts and the opportunity log. Keep client and personal-offer outcomes separate. Recommend one thing to repeat and one thing to change, with the evidence for each.

## Review the draft and its results

### Before publishing

Apply the [editorial pass](#editorial-pass), then check these points without producing a long checklist unless requested:

1. **Reader and point.** Can the intended person recognize the situation? Does the post make one useful contribution?
2. **Claim integrity.** Which statements come from the user, an inspected source, an inference, or a plan? Are numbers, time windows, denominators, and qualifications intact? Does "I" refer to something this person actually did?
3. **Angle fit.** A demo needs a working artifact. A benchmark needs measurements. A trend needs a comparison. A forecast needs a future test. A series needs a useful installment. Use the specific requirements in the angle library.
4. **Useful detail.** Include the action, mechanism, constraint, or consequence that makes the point distinct. Remove sentences that could describe almost any professional.
5. **Voice after editing.** Preserve the user's tone and certainty. Confirm that the editorial pass removed filler without removing a useful technical term, altering a quotation, flattening the voice, or strengthening a claim. Do not add an editing report to ordinary post output.
6. **Proof and length.** Confirm that the proposed proof exists or is marked as still needed. Check account capability, preferred format, and the final length of each post or thread part. Longer copy must retain useful substance, not padding. Verify placeholders have been replaced before marking final copy ready. Private illustrative previews are ready to evaluate as examples, not evidence of actual work or results.
7. **Next step.** The ask should fit the reader and the primary outcome. Advice and observations can stand on their own. Avoid multiple competing asks.

Return one of three states with a precise reason:

- **Ready for user review:** the draft is supported by the supplied material and any required asset is available.
- **Needs a fact:** an outcome, method, permission-relevant disclosure, actual availability, or other material statement remains unresolved.
- **Needs an asset:** the copy relies on a demo, screenshot, example, or resource that still has to be prepared.

If the post can remain accurate without the missing element, remove the dependency and deliver that version. A concept that explains a proposed test can be ready as a concept. It must not become a shipped-project claim through editing.

### After publishing

Use [the log](#post-and-opportunity-log). Read engagement and commercial outcomes separately.

| Stage | CLIENTS | OFFERS |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No observed qualified client evidence after review | No observed qualified role evidence after review |
| 1 | Identifiable buyer asks about a real scoped project | Identifiable person approaches the author about a real role or assignment |
| 2 | Relevant discovery or project call is booked | Relevant call or interview is booked |
| 3 | Signed or otherwise documented paid engagement | Documented offer, signed contract, or hire |

Keep the evidence level visible. A self-reported win is an author report. Public replies often show only the beginning of a conversation. Exact-post attribution requires the prospect or another source to connect the opportunity to that post. Do not convert general account visibility into exact-post conversion.

Record stage progression for each unique opportunity. Show counts as well as highest stage, so one signed engagement does not hide ten inquiries. When stage 3 means an offer was received, do not silently relabel it as an accepted role.

For each review, answer three questions: Which readers engaged? What real opportunities appeared? What evidence or question should the next post address? Repeat promising angles with different real material. One post per angle is an exploratory trial, not a ranking of causal effects. Differences in topic, proof, timing, audience size, and distribution can explain results.

If the user wants to compare two writing approaches, choose one difference at a time where practical and record the other differences. Qualified opportunities may take weeks. Review them again after the initial engagement snapshots instead of assuming seven days is the final outcome window.

## Worked examples

These examples demonstrate decisions about reader, evidence, angle, and format. They are not facts to reuse in another person's profile. The short Nidrosoft example uses user-supplied context and public descriptions. The other profiles and explicitly illustrative scenarios are teaching material, not verified accounts of the author's work.

The two Premium format demonstrations below use invented scenarios, explicitly identified outside the copy. They demonstrate narrative depth and thread structure, not actual client outcomes or the author's experience. Short examples later in this document remain useful when the reader and material call for them.

### Premium long post: a pattern observation

**Illustrative profile and material:** An AI architect observed three imagined support pilots. The intended reader is an engineering lead considering the author for a personal assignment. The repeated work of checking facts supports A08. This is a private-preview teaching example, not a verified case study.

**Format:** One connected long post. The observation and proposed next experiment benefit from being read together. No sales ask or artificial thread split is necessary.

**Post:**

> Elena's support team could generate a reply in seconds and still needed four systems open before sending it.
>
> The order status was in one place. Delivery information was in another. Refund rules lived in a document. The conversation history was in the support tool.
>
> The draft arrived quickly. Checking whether it was correct took the work.
>
> I saw the same pattern in two other support pilots: faster writing exposed how much time people spent finding and verifying the facts.
>
> That changes what I'd measure in the next experiment.
>
> I'd follow a ticket from opening it to sending an answer someone had checked. I'd record which systems the person visited, what they corrected, and what they still had to decide.
>
> Then I'd test whether providing current order details and the relevant policy reduced that work.
>
> There will still be cases where a person needs to make a judgment. I want those cases visible.
>
> A useful support benchmark should tell me whether the team reached a correct, reviewable answer with less effort. Draft generation time is one part of that measurement.

**Readiness:** Complete as an illustrative format example. Real publication as a firsthand observation requires the author's actual sessions and findings. Preserve the bounded scope and proposed-test language when adapting it.

### Premium thread: an engineering case study

**Illustrative profile and material:** A product engineer explains an imagined import repair to an engineering lead. A03 fits the actual decision sequence within the scenario. The named person and implementation history are invented teaching material.

**Format:** Four numbered posts. Each adds a different part of the explanation: the trigger, architecture, retry behavior, and verification. Premium access permits longer parts; a standard-account adaptation would need different splits.

**Post 1 of 4:**

> 1/4
>
> Evan closed his laptop during a customer import, and the import stopped halfway through.
>
> I had built the first version around an open browser session. It worked during a demonstration because nobody closed the tab.
>
> Once customers started importing larger files while doing other work, that assumption stopped holding.
>
> I moved the import into a background worker.

**Post 2 of 4:**

> 2/4
>
> The server now creates an import record, stores the file, and queues the work.
>
> The browser receives a job ID and displays progress from that record.
>
> Closing the page no longer owns the fate of the import. When Evan opens it again, the page retrieves the same job and shows its current state.
>
> That also gives support a specific job to inspect when someone reports a problem.

**Post 3 of 4:**

> 3/4
>
> Moving the work introduced another question: what happens when a worker retries a batch?
>
> I gave each imported row a stable identity within its import and enforced uniqueness in the database.
>
> That protects against the same logical row being inserted twice, including when two attempts overlap.
>
> Progress only advances after the batch has been committed.

**Post 4 of 4:**

> 4/4
>
> I tested closing the tab, refreshing during an import, and interrupting the worker between batches.
>
> The job resumed, and the imported rows stayed unique.
>
> Cancellation is the next piece. A queued job needs a stop request the worker can observe; closing a browser tab can't serve that purpose anymore.
>
> I'm keeping that distinction visible in the interface so "close" and "cancel" don't promise the same behavior.

**Readiness:** Complete as an illustrative thread. A real case study must use the author's actual architecture, tests, and remaining work. The copy does not promise an attached recording or repository.

### Nidrosoft and a delivered integration

**Profile supplied for this example:** Cyriac runs Nidrosoft and describes 15+ years across product engineering, full-stack development, AI architecture, design, founding, consulting, and forward-deployed engineering. The personal account should support both studio projects and individual opportunities.

**Brief:** A published client testimonial describes an HVAC owner working on roofs while calls and lead details were being missed. Nidrosoft built a phone-agent and CRM workflow that captures the job and alerts the owner. The intended reader is a business owner with the same problem. The goal for this post is CLIENTS. A public testimonial is available, but a demonstration recording has not been supplied.

**Facts available:** The client's reported situation and workflow are described on [Nidrosoft's website](https://nidrosoft.com/). There is no supplied numerical revenue, booking, or time-saving result. The author's experience comes from the author's current statement, rather than an independently checked employment history.

**Choice:** A02 Client deliverable showcase. The material describes a real engagement and an operational change. It does not support a benchmark or a numerical before-and-after claim.

**Draft:**

> An HVAC owner was missing leads while working on roofs.
>
> I built a phone agent connected to his CRM. It captures the job and alerts him while he's still on site.
>
> If your leads disappear between a call and a callback, let's talk about it at Nidrosoft.

**Supporting material:** Link the published testimonial. A demonstration with sample data would help, but the copy does not claim that a video is attached.

**Readiness:** Ready for the author's review against the source and actual scope. No fabricated conversion metric. If the copy is changed to promise a recording, readiness changes to Needs an asset until that recording exists.

**Same use case, different reader:** For an engineering team evaluating the author personally, A13 can show a diagnostic without inventing implementation details:

> If your form says "sent" but no lead reaches the CRM, submit one test with a unique name.
>
> Trace it through the form, server, integration and CRM.
>
> Investigate the first missing record. Check what was saved before trusting the success message.

The topic is still an integration. The contribution changed from showing delivered client work to helping diagnose a failure. This second draft does not claim that the HVAC system had that particular bug.

### A software engineer with an architecture idea

**Fictional profile:** A backend engineer wants contract or full-time opportunities at teams building data-heavy products. They are thinking through import reliability and have not supplied a shipped implementation or benchmark.

**Brief:** Explain when a CSV import should run as a background job. The intended reader is an engineering lead. Outcome is OFFERS. Available material is reasoning about cancellation, progress, and recovery after a disconnect. No measured latency or customer result is available.

**Choice:** A14 Decision rule. There is enough material for a conditional recommendation, but not a shipped demo or a postmortem.

**Draft:**

> I'd keep a small, bounded import in the request.
>
> When users need cancellation, progress or recovery after a disconnect, I'd move it to a background job.
>
> The design question is who owns the work after the browser closes.

**Supporting material:** Optional sketch of request ownership and background-job ownership. It must be labeled as a design proposal if created.

**Readiness:** Ready for review as a recommendation. No assertion that the engineer has shipped a queue, reduced latency, or solved an outage. No sales CTA is necessary for this post.

### An independent small-business consultant

**Fictional profile:** A consultant helps service businesses improve intake and scheduling. They want scoped client engagements through their practice. They have no performance study to cite.

**Brief:** Help an owner investigate appointments that are confirmed to customers but missing from the team's calendar. Give them a next check that can reveal where the process fails.

**Choice:** A13 Public help and diagnosis. Use a conditional symptom, rather than claiming the consultant found the cause inside a particular client's system.

**Draft:**

> If a customer has a booking confirmation but your team can't find the appointment, trace one test booking.
>
> Check the form, calendar and notification separately.
>
> A confirmation email tells you what was sent. Verify which calendar received the booking.

**Supporting material:** A simple diagram can help. The diagnostic is useful without a downloadable resource or invented case study.

**Readiness:** Ready for review as conditional advice. A follow-up can explain a real investigation once it has happened.

### An early-career designer moving into engineering

**Fictional profile:** A designer is learning frontend implementation and wants personal offers. They have no senior title, client results, or employer history to cite. They can offer a short component handoff checklist.

**Brief:** Help a teammate implement form behavior consistently. The resource is the content of the post itself.

**Choice:** A06 Free tool or template. The artifact exists inline. No GitHub release or years-of-experience claim is needed.

**Draft:**

> A form handoff checklist you can paste into a component spec:
>
> [ ] Loading, success and error states
> [ ] Values kept after a failed submission
> [ ] Keyboard path and error focus
> [ ] Small-screen behavior with long input

**Supporting material:** Optional filled example for one component. If the writer has only tried it on a learning project, keep that context when discussing results.

**Readiness:** Ready for review as an inline checklist. The author can demonstrate useful knowledge without borrowing someone else's seniority.

### A requested comparison without test results

**Request:** "Write a post saying model A is better than model B at customer support. I have not tested them yet."

**Choice:** A11 is the intended angle, but it needs measurements. Do not manufacture them or silently describe a test as complete. Offer a proposed experiment.

**Draft:**

> My proposed support-model test would use the same held-out tickets, tools and scoring rules for each model.
>
> I'd compare verified answers, escalation quality and cost per resolved ticket, then publish the failures alongside the totals.

**Readiness:** Ready for review as a proposed test. A result-bearing comparison still needs the actual experiment, versions, conditions, denominator, and measurements.

## Research context and limits

This framework began with two purpose-built X research samples collected for Nidrosoft in November 2026. The first retained 10,000 posts from 3,413 accounts below 25,000 followers at collection. A second retained 12,000 additional unique posts from 8,731 accounts under the same follower threshold and broadened the subjects beyond design. Counts describe collection, not the number of posts deeply read by a person.

The second pass used 24 individually reviewed examples, three per proposed new angle, to develop the eight additions. An initial 32-row check found only nine matching automated angle labels, with the remainder differently classified or off-scope. Automated tags were therefore useful for discovery rather than reliable prevalence estimates. The samples were selected through searches and author timelines, not random samples of X.

The initial seven editorial slots were shipped demos, client deliverables, process case studies, independent redesigns, specific availability, resources, and numbered series. The eight additions were observation, data-backed trend, field note, controlled comparison, failure postmortem, public diagnosis, decision rule, and testable forecast. These choices are a usable writing library. They are not an exhaustive taxonomy or fifteen independently proven conversion methods.

Public commercial-outcome evidence was limited. The first study recorded four client cases and three offer cases tied to an exact post or named format. The second found one format-linked, author-reported qualified client inquiry and no format-linked personal offer in its new sample. Other account-level and cross-platform reports do not establish exact-post conversion. A lack of public evidence does not establish that no private outcome occurred.

Large reference accounts can illustrate craft. Their reach does not predict the performance of a small account. Do not market the kit as "22,000 manually analyzed viral posts" or as a guarantee of work. The user's own qualified opportunities should guide future revisions.

### Sources that illustrate the distinctions

- [Emil Kowalski on animating in public](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/animating-in-public) is a firsthand account connecting public work with career opportunities. It is an example, not a universal effect size.
- [A publicly shared redesign case](https://x.com/lizengco/status/1757953166837252468) illustrates a creator's report of work arising from a redesign. Retain the author-report status.
- [Karpathy's original vibe-coding post](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383) illustrates naming a behavior from a personal experience. Treat a large account as a style reference.
- [Anthropic's long-running agent experiments](https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) describe specific observed failures and engineering responses. They can support a bounded observation about those experiments.
- [Census AI use by business size](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html) provides a population, measurement question, time period, and comparison. Keep its December 2025 to May 2026 scope when using it.

The underlying research workbooks and raw exports are separate from this lightweight kit. If someone requests a new research claim, inspect the relevant sources or data rather than using these aggregate counts as substantiation.

### Refreshing responsibly

Use live research when requested or when the intended claim depends on current facts. Treg is one optional route when connected. Discover the endpoint and its price before metered use. Save source URLs, dates, query origin, and available metrics. Deduplicate posts and keep missing values blank. Review example labels and commercial evidence before making comparisons. Distinguish an author report, an identifiable inquiry, a booked conversation, and a signed outcome. Keep exact-post, series, and account-level attribution separate.
